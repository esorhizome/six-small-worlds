| 0:20–1:00 | Presenter or voice over the running build. Episode title appears as plain text, lower third. | Welcome back to Six Small Worlds. | 1:00–2:20 | *B-roll: a rope coiled flat on a wooden deck, overhead shot. Then a chambered nautilus shell in cross-section.* | Start with no maths at all. Here's a rope coiled on a deck. Every ring sits against the last, and every ring is the same thickness — because rope doesn't grow. Measure the gap between rings anywhere in that coil: same answer. Now look at this shell. The creature inside built it, room by room, at the opening — and the creature was bigger each season than the season before. So each new turn of shell isn't the same *amount* bigger. It's bigger *in proportion*. D'Arcy Thompson put it beautifully over a century ago: a rope is a coiled cylinder. A shell is a coiled cone. |
| 2:20–3:30 | *B-roll: photo of Jacob Bernoulli's tombstone in Basel (MacTutor hosts one).* Text on screen: "Eadem mutata resurgo". | Even professionals confuse these two. Jacob Bernoulli studied the growing spiral in the 1690s and loved it so much he named it spira mirabilis — the marvellous spiral. He asked for it to be carved on his tombstone, with a motto: though changed, I rise again the same. The stonemason did his best. And carved the *rope* spiral instead. It's still there in Basel. Three hundred years of being politely wrong. By the end of this episode, you'd catch it from across the room. |
| 3:30–4:40 | Whiteboard-style graphic: a dot, an arrow outward (r), an arc (θ). Degrees marked. | To draw any spiral we need two numbers. How far from the centre — call it r, the radius. And how far around we've turned — call it theta. That's it. That's polar coordinates. If you built World 1, these are the same two dials as sin and cos, wearing outdoor clothes. We'll talk in degrees while we think, and the code will use radians — I'll flag the switch when it happens. |
| 4:40–5:40 | The Archimedean formula appears: r = a + b·θ. Underneath, in words: "start at a; every bit of turn ADDS the same bit of distance". | Spiral number one is about twenty-two centuries old. Archimedes, in a book called On Spirals, around 225 BC: move a point outward at a steady speed while the line it rides turns at a steady speed. As a formula: r equals a plus b theta. In words — start at a, and every bit of turning adds the same bit of distance. Adding. That's the whole personality. Equal gaps, forever. Rope, clock spring, liquorice wheel. |
| 5:40–7:40 | Live Godot: new project, Node2D, attach script. Paste milestone 1. Run. The rope coil appears. *B-roll cutaway: the coiled rope again, matching the render.* | Let's build it. One node, one script — everything in this course is paste-and-run. The pen walks around in two-degree steps; at each step we ask the formula for r, then cos and sin turn "angle and distance" into a screen position. There's the switch I promised: deg_to_rad at the door, radians inside, once. And run. Five rings, even gaps. If yours is lopsided or half off screen — you're ninety per cent of the way there. Lopsided means it's drawing. Nudge the constants at the top; they're yours. |
| 7:40–8:50 | The logarithmic formula appears: r = a·e^(b·θ). Underneath: "start at a; every bit of turn MULTIPLIES the distance". | Spiral number two changes one operation. r equals a times e to the b theta. That e is Euler's number, about 2.718 — the mathematics of compounding. In words: start at a, and every bit of turning *multiplies* the distance by the same amount. Not equal gaps. Equal *ratios*. With the pace set to 0.11 per radian, each full turn very nearly doubles the radius. The shell doubles every lap, calmly, because the animal inside kept growing. |
| 8:50–10:40 | Live Godot: paste the full starter. Run. Both spirals, labelled. Slow zoom on each in turn. | Here's the full file — both spirals, side by side, with labels. The only genuinely new line is exp: e raised to b theta, the multiplying engine. Run it. Left: the rope. Finished, even, content. Right: starts as a tight whisper and opens like a door. Look between them until the difference stops being a fact and becomes a feeling. The left one is done. The right one is going somewhere. That's the mark of life: the constant ratio. |
| 10:40–12:20 | *B-roll: the classic "golden spiral over a nautilus" poster image, then Falbo's numbers as text: 1.24–1.43, average 1.33 — not 1.618.* Live: set LOG_GROWTH to 0.3064 and TURNS to 2, run; then 0.18, run. | Now the myth-check, because this repo has a rule: sources or it didn't happen. You've seen the poster — a nautilus with a golden spiral drawn over it, claiming the shell grows by phi, 1.618, every quarter turn. In 2005 Clement Falbo published actual measurements of actual nautilus shells. The ratios ran from 1.24 to 1.43. Average: about 1.33. Not phi. Not close. Watch — here's the golden pace in our own code: 0.3064. I have to drop to two turns because at that pace two turns already multiply the radius about forty-seven times. See how fast it flees? Now Falbo's measured pace: 0.18. Cosier. Slower. That's the animal. The nautilus is a true logarithmic spiral — which is already wonderful — and it is not the golden spiral. |
| 12:20–13:10 | Text on screen: "You can stop here." The three check-yourself questions appear one by one. | And here is this episode's marked line: you can stop here. Both spirals drawn, myth retired — that's the whole promise, kept. Before you go, three questions, answers in the lesson: equal gaps of 22 pixels — which family? A creature that doubles every season — which spiral is its diary? And: true or false, the nautilus is a golden spiral? |
| 13:10–13:55 | Go-deeper prompts on screen. *B-roll: the golden-pace spiral escaping the frame, looped.* | If you want more: the lesson's go-deeper section has you draw the myth and the animal back to back, and asks a question worth sitting with — why did the turn count have to shrink when the pace grew? What does that say about multiplication? And an open prompt: design the calmest spiral in the pool, and defend it to a friend. |
| 13:55–14:10 | **End card.** Both spirals running. Next-episode title fades in: "E08 · Visitors while you're away". | So: two spirals exist on your screen, and you know which one is alive. Next episode, the pool learns to receive guests while you're gone — a month of them, in one second. And one breath of the real game: the shelled visitors in Tidepool Keeper wear the right-hand spiral. The one that grows. See you at the next tide. |

## E08 · Visitors while you're away

**Lesson:** [lesson-2-visitors-while-youre-away.md](lesson-2-visitors-while-youre-away.md) ·
**Starter:** [starter/visits.gd](starter/visits.gd) · **Runtime target:** ~14–15 min

**Cold open (0:00–0:20).** The Output panel pouring a month of guestbook
lines in real time — one second of cascade — ending on "=== Nothing expired
while you were gone. ===". No logo.

| Time | On screen | Spoken |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | The console cascade, then the closing line holds. *B-roll: none — the text is the visual.* | I was away for a month. This is what my tidepool did about it. A hundred and twenty visitors, timestamped, none of them demanded of me — and the whole month took one second to happen. |
| 0:20–1:05 | Title lower-third. Then plain text: "no streaks · no caps · nothing expires". | Welcome back to Six Small Worlds, World 3: Tidepool Keeper. Today's promise: you'll simulate a month of guestbook entries in a blink, using one line of honest mathematics. And a design promise underneath it: this is the away-time mechanic — uncapped, guilt-free, the opposite of a login streak. The maths is what makes the kindness affordable. Marked stopping point as always; stopping counts. |
| 1:05–2:25 | *B-roll: rain on a single paving stone, real footage, 20 seconds of it. Drops cluster, then pause.* | No code yet. Watch rain hit one paving stone. You cannot say when the next drop lands. But you can say roughly how many land per minute. And look at the rhythm — two drops almost together, then a dry spell, then three in a row. That clustering is not broken rain. That's what independent events at a steady average genuinely look like. Hold onto that footage; we're about to make the computer do exactly this. |
| 2:25–3:35 | Big text: λ. Underneath: "the average rate — visitors per hour". Worked line: 4 per day = 1 per 6 hours. | One symbol first. Lambda — the average rate. Raindrops per minute; visitors per hour. It's the single number that says how busy the pool is. We'll use four visitors a day, which is one every six hours, on average. On average is the load-bearing phrase. No visitor checks a schedule. |
| 3:35–5:00 | The formula, built up piece by piece: gap = −ln(u) / λ. Each symbol highlighted as it's spoken. Worked examples appear: u=0.9 → 38 min; u=0.5 → 4.2 h; u=0.05 → 18 h. | Here's the whole trick. The gap until the next visit is: minus the natural log of u, divided by lambda. Symbol by symbol. u is a random fraction between zero and one — in Godot, randf. ln is the natural logarithm — the un-doing of e from last episode; in GDScript it's spelled log. ln of a fraction is negative, so the minus sign flips it into a positive wait. And dividing by lambda scales it into hours. Feel it with numbers: u lands near one — gap of thirty-eight minutes, drops clustering. u lands at a half — four hours. u lands way down at 0.05 — an eighteen-hour dry spell. Most gaps short, a few long, average exactly one over lambda. Mathematicians call the gaps exponential, and arrivals built from them a Poisson process. Grinstead and Snell's free textbook has the full story — link below. |
| 5:00–6:20 | Live Godot: milestone 1, ten gaps printed. Run. Run again. Two different columns of numbers side by side. | Ten lines of code, ten gaps. One small kindness to the computer first: randf can, on a rare day, return exactly zero, and log of zero is a question with no answer. So we clamp it — never hand the log a zero. Run. Ten uneven numbers, average vaguely six. Run again — completely different numbers, same texture. If your numbers match nobody else's: correct. That's the entire point. |
| 6:20–8:00 | Live Godot: milestone 2. The accumulate loop highlighted. Run: the month pours. Scroll through it slowly. | Now the month. Gaps become arrival times by adding them up — first visit at gap one, second at gap one plus gap two, and so on until thirty days run out. Format the hours into Day, hour, minute... and run. There it is. Around a hundred and twenty entries — some days five visits, some days one, the odd day none at all. Run it again and the total wobbles. That wobble is randomness being truthful. We promised the player an average; the sea keeps the details. |
| 8:00–9:30 | *B-roll: NOAA's lunar-day diagram; then an archive photo of a brass tide-predicting machine.* Text: "high tides ~12 h 25 min apart · M2 ≈ 12.42 h". | The guestbook works. But this is a *tide*pool, and tides run on the strangest clock in daily life. Most coasts get two high tides per lunar day — and a lunar day is twenty-four hours *fifty minutes*, because the moon drifts along its orbit while the Earth spins underneath it. So highs arrive about twelve hours twenty-five minutes apart — that's NOAA's education pages, linked below. Oceanographers call the main lunar ingredient M2, period twelve point four two hours. And the idea of predicting real tides by adding simple waves like that one goes back to William Thomson — later Lord Kelvin — who began harmonic tide prediction in 1867, eventually with brass and gears. Our pool needs exactly one of his waves. |
| 9:30–10:50 | Live Godot: milestone 3. tide_height and tide_word added. Run. Entries now read "...at high water, when the pool brims". | One sine wave with the M2 period — plus one small switch to announce: we wrote it with 360 degrees on the whiteboard, but sin in code wants radians, so a full circle is TAU. That's the whole conversion. Now every guestbook line carries the water it arrived on. And notice — scroll with me — the high-water entries drift about fifty minutes later each day. We never programmed that drift. It fell out of the period. The moon does the same thing to real beaches. |
| 10:50–12:30 | Live Godot: the final starter with _draw. Run: teal wave, week one, dots riding it. Slow pan across the timeline. | Last step: a picture. A month of tide wiggles is fifty-eight cycles — that's texture, not a picture. One week is thirteen and a half — that's a picture. So: print the month, draw week one. The wave is the tide; every dot is a guest, sitting at exactly the water height it arrived on. Clusters and quiet stretches — the rain on the stone, drawn. If your dots bunch oddly, run it again. Some weeks are like that. Both of you are correct. |
| 12:30–13:20 | Text: "You can stop here." Then go-deeper prompts. | The marked line came earlier than usual today — the moment the month first printed, you'd already finished. Everything since was sea-flavoured bonus. If you want more: the lesson shows *thinning* — keep each visit with a probability that follows the tide, so guests genuinely favour high water. And an open design prompt: a player returns after ninety days to three hundred and sixty entries. Design the kindest three-line welcome-back summary. What do you tell them first? |
| 13:20–14:20 | The three check-yourself questions on screen, one by one. *B-roll: the paving stone again, briefly.* | Three questions before the tide turns. Lambda is two per hour — what's the average gap? Which draw gives the longer wait, u at 0.9 or u at 0.1 — and why? And where do the extra twenty-five minutes between high tides come from? Answers at the bottom of the lesson, folded away where you can't see them by accident. |
| 14:20–14:35 | **End card.** The week timeline breathing. Next-episode title: "E09 · The thank-you dance". | So: a month of visits exists in one second, on a clock set by the moon. Next episode, the creatures say thank you — by drawing. And the breath of the real game, this week, is the whole point of the episode: this is the guestbook you come home to in Tidepool Keeper. Away a month, nothing expired, everyone signed. See you at the next tide. |

| Time | On screen | Spoken |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | The mandala drawing itself, unhurried. *B-roll: none. Hold the shot.* | This is a creature saying thank you. It's going to take about six seconds, and it will not hurry, and that's the design. |
| 0:20–1:00 | Title lower-third over the running dance. | Welcome to the last episode of World 3. The promise: by the end, a creature draws its signature pattern above the water — one shy petal for a stranger, the full mandala for a best friend — growing, and slowly turning, with friendship. This is the reward that is also the collection. Marked stopping point as always. |
| 1:00–2:10 | *B-roll: a hand signing a birthday card — initials first, then a full flourish with a postscript.* | No code yet. Think about how a friend signs a card. The signature is always recognisably theirs — same loops, same slant. What changes is how much they write. A colleague gets initials. An old friend gets the flourish and a postscript. Tidepool Keeper's creatures work exactly like that: every species has one pattern it draws its whole life. Friendship decides how much of it you're trusted with. |
| 2:10–3:20 | The rose formula appears: r = cos(k·θ). A rose blooms beside it as k counts 3, 5, 7. Text: "odd k → k petals · even k → 2k". | The pattern is a rose curve — if you grew the Garden in World 2, this is your flower, back for a reunion. r equals cos of k theta: as the pen turns, its distance from the centre breathes in and out, k times per lap. Every breath is a petal. Odd k gives exactly k petals; even k gives two k. They're called rhodonea — Guido Grandi named them in the 1720s because they looked like roses, which remains the correct reaction. Each species in our pool gets its own k. A signature number. |
| 3:20–4:40 | Live Godot: milestone 1. One five-petal rose, still. Change k to 7, run, count. Back to 5. | Let's draw one, whole and holding still. One node, one script, twenty lines. The pen sweeps half a turn — 180 degrees, which the code calls PI radians, and that's today's whole degrees-to-radians switch. Run: five petals. Change the species number to seven: seven petals, a different signature. Count them — counting is allowed, this is the beginner-friendly channel. Back to five before the next step, so our petal arithmetic stays tidy. |
| 4:40–5:50 | Whiteboard graphic: a 180° arc divided into five slices; slices fill as f goes 1→5. | Now friendship. Five levels. The move is lovely and small: friendship decides how far theta is allowed to travel. Level one gets one-fifth of the pattern. Level five gets all of it. And with k equal to five, the arithmetic aligns perfectly — each friendship level is exactly one more petal. The pattern doesn't change. Your *access* to it does. |
| 5:50–7:10 | Live Godot: milestone 2. Run at friendship 1, then 3, then 5 — three screenshots side by side in edit. | Code it: theta runs to friendship over five, times PI — and size grows with friendship too, because a level-five thank-you should fill more of the sky. Run at one: a single small petal. A creature that met you this morning. At three: three petals, larger. At five: the whole rose. You're looking at a relationship in three screenshots. |
| 7:10–8:20 | Slow zoom on the still level-5 rose. Text: "a drawing that arrives is a dance". | One thing missing. A drawing that appears all at once is a diagram. A drawing that *arrives* is a dance. So we give the pen a speed — half a radian of theta per second — and let time reveal the curve. Plus one more gentleness: the finished shape turns, slowly, a few degrees a second, like something catching the light. Growth says our friendship is bigger. The spin says this moment is alive. |
| 8:20–10:20 | Live Godot: the final starter. Both dances running. Hold on the right dance completing. *B-roll: cut back to the cold open shot — now the viewer knows what they're seeing.* | The full build: two dances share the screen. Left, friendship one. Right, friendship five. The pen's position is elapsed time times tempo — but never past what friendship allows; minf is the chaperone. And the spin is added to the position angle only, so the rose rotates rigidly instead of morphing. Run. The left dance finishes its petal in about a second and rests, turning gently. Polite, brief, complete. The right one is still going... petal after petal, same tempo, about six seconds, because there's five times more of it and it declines to hurry. Don't touch anything. Watch one mandala arrive. That patience is the mechanic. |
| 10:20–11:30 | Design card: "friendship = more of the same beauty". *B-roll: mock journal page — five stages of one signature, fifth slot empty.* | Notice what the reward is *not*. It's not a number going up. Best-friend status pays you in more of the same beauty — larger, longer, slower. In the finished game these drawings live in the Pattern Journal: a naturalist's notebook, one page per species, filled in as friendship grows. And because this studio doesn't do streaks, the empty slots never threaten you. They wait. An empty page is a promise, not a deadline. |
| 11:30–12:40 | Go-deeper prompts on screen: k=4 with TAU; k=2.5 with 720°; the tempo question. | Bonus paths, if you want them. Even signatures: k of four draws *eight* petals, but needs a full turn of room. Stranger ones: k of two and a half needs *two* full turns — 720 degrees — to find its way home; some species are like that. And the question I'd sit with: suppose you sped the pen up for higher levels so every dance lasted one second flat. What exactly would be lost? Whom would the dance be for, then? |
| 12:40–13:30 | The three check-yourself questions. Then a recap strip: E07 spirals · E08 guestbook · E09 dance. | Three questions: k equals seven — how many petals? Friendship three of five, with k five — how many appear? And why does the best-friend dance run longer when the pen speed never changed? Answers folded at the bottom of the lesson. And look at what three episodes built: shells that grow by ratio. A month of guests on a moon clock. And a thank-you drawn in petals. That's an away-time visit system — the genre's kindest mechanic, and now you've built its heart. |
| 13:30–13:45 | **End card.** Both dances running. Next-world title: "World 4 · An Isolate Grows Roots — E10: Teach a turtle to walk". | So: one creature, two friendships, both dances on your screen.
