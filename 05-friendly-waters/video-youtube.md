# Friendly Waters — long-form scripts (E13–E15)

*World 5 of the [long-form course](../00-course/longform-youtube.md). Three
episodes, 12–18 minutes of speech each; one written lesson per episode is the
skeleton, this file is the performance. Batch-record all three in one sitting.
House reminders: no logo before the cold open's visual; conversational
register — shorter sentences than the written lessons; B-roll cues in
italics; the last 15 seconds are always the end card.*

## E13 · Why the deep is dark

*Lesson skeleton: [lesson-1-why-the-deep-is-dark.md](lesson-1-why-the-deep-is-dark.md)
· Starter: [starter/depth_light.gd](starter/depth_light.gd) · Target ~14:00*

**Cold open (0:00–0:20).** Screen-recorded finished build: the glow at full
size in tropical blue, then a slow descent — the circle collapsing, zone names
sliding past, the readout growing zeros. One line, spoken over it:

> "This fading light is a law written in 1729 — and one small game's entire
> economy."

**Beats.**

| Time | On screen | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0:20–1:05 | Presenter or voice over the finished build, paused mid-Twilight. | "Hello, and welcome to Friendly Waters — world five of six. Over three episodes we're building an ocean: today the light, next time the water, then the company. Here's the whole promise of today: by the end, you'll have a light that fades with depth *honestly* — the way real seawater does it. One equation. And the usual house rule stands: there's a marked stopping point, and stopping there counts as finishing." |
| 1:05–2:20 | *B-roll: car headlights in fog at night; torch beam in mist.* | "Before any symbols — headlights in fog. Near the car, fierce. A few metres out, soft. Further, a suggestion. Then nothing. No wall stopped that light. Every metre of fog took a cut. That's the feeling to hold on to: not a wall — a tax." |
| 2:20–3:40 | Whiteboard-style: 100 → 50 → 25 → 12 → 6, equal steps down the screen. Then beside it: 100 → 75 → 50 → 25 → 0. | "Two ways light could fade. Option one: lose the same *amount* each metre. A hundred, seventy-five, fifty, twenty-five, zero. Done. Hits the floor. Option two: keep the same *fraction* each metre. A hundred, fifty, twenty-five, twelve and a half... Notice it never actually lands on zero — it thins forever. Water does option two. The ocean charges a percentage, never a flat fee." |
| 3:40–5:00 | The maths box from the lesson: I = I₀ · e^(−k·z), symbols appearing one at a time. | "Here's the law, and it's smaller than it looks. I — the light that remains. I-nought — the light you started with at the surface. k — the water's appetite: what fraction each metre eats. z — your depth. And e is Euler's number, about 2.718 — the eˣ button on any scientific calculator. It's how maths says 'apply the same multiplier continuously' without listing every metre. Read the whole thing aloud: the light that remains is the light you started with, times the survival fraction, once for every metre down." |
| 5:00–5:45 | *B-roll: engraving-style portraits or title pages: Bouguer 1729, Lambert 1760, Beer 1852.* | "Quick lineage, because this law has three parents. Pierre Bouguer, 1729: each layer of a transparent stuff keeps the same fraction of light. Lambert restates it in 1760. Beer, 1852, ties the constant to what's dissolved in the liquid. Chemists say Beer–Lambert. History says: Bouguer got there first." |
| 5:45–6:45 | Godot editor, live: Step 1 code pasted, F5. A pale disc in blue. Then Step 2: hold ↓, the depth counter climbs; the sun ignores it. | "Let's build. New project, one Node2D, one script — everything this course ever needs. Step one: a sun in the water. Step two: a number called depth that you control — hold down, it climbs, and clampf keeps it between the surface and the seabed at ten thousand nine hundred and thirty-five metres. Notice the light doesn't care yet. The ocean is broken, and we know exactly why." |
| 6:45–8:00 | Step 3 diff moment — but shown as the full file, with the one load-bearing line highlighted: `var survival := exp(-K_PER_METRE * depth)`. Run: descend slowly; the disc collapses. | "Step three is the whole episode. One line: survival equals exp of minus k times depth. A number between one and nearly zero. Multiply the radius by it. Run... and descend. Look at that. Big losses immediately — half gone every thirty metres, a tenth left each hundred. By two hundred metres, one percent. This is the moment the toy becomes honest." |
| 8:00–9:00 | Step 4: water colour lerped by survival; the glow gets its three soft layers. Side-by-side before/after. | "Two touches, same idea worn twice. The water's colour fades by the *same* survival number — one law, everything obeys it. And the poker-chip disc becomes three stacked circles, wide and faint to small and bright, so it reads as light. If your blues look different from mine — good. You found the knobs." |
| 9:00–10:15 | Step 5: the DEPTH_MARKS array on screen; then running, zone lines drifting past. *B-roll overlay: NOAA-style zone diagram, surface to trench.* | "Last step: the real ocean's floor plan, from NOAA's education pages. Sunlight zone to two hundred metres. Twilight to a thousand. Midnight to four thousand — sunlight effectively gone. The Abyss to six thousand. And the Hadal zone: the trenches, down to about eleven kilometres. Each boundary is an entry in one array, at its true depth, drifting past as you sink." |
| 10:15–11:15 | The full descent, performed mostly in silence: glow dies before Twilight ends; names pass in the dark; readout shows `1.02e-08 %`, then longer tails; Challenger Deep line rises to meet the centre. | "Now the full trip. Watch the readout once the glow is gone... that's scientific notation — the maths writing zeros for us. A ten-billionth of the surface light at the Midnight line. And this — ten thousand nine hundred and thirty-five metres. Challenger Deep. Most of the ocean you own is water the sun has never meaningfully reached. You now know why, and by which equation." |
| 11:15–11:50 | STOP HERE card over the running build. | "And you can stop here. Genuinely. A light that fades by the real law, through the real zones, to the real bottom — that's the promise, kept. If yours only mostly works — if the glow dies but the labels are wonky — you are ninety percent of the way there, and the labels are cosmetics." |
| 11:50–12:50 | Go-deeper montage: three glows (red, green, blue) dying in order; a fantasy-k run where Midnight keeps a little sky. *B-roll: deep-sea footage, blue-black water; a pale snailfish near the seafloor.* | "Bonus paths, no obligation. Colours die in order — water eats red first, blue last, which is why underwater film goes blue-green; give each colour its own k and watch your sun die in colour order. Or bend the law on purpose: k equals zero point zero zero two grows a fantasy sea. And — somebody lives down there. The deepest fishes known are hadal snailfishes; one was described from the Mariana Trench, from depths approaching eight kilometres. Hold that thought until episode fifteen." |
| 12:50–13:45 | *Friendly Waters* mood frame (store-page level): a small bright figure descending, her glow the only lit thing. | "Why does a game studio care about an attenuation law? Because of what it makes *legible*. In Friendly Waters, Volta is an introvert with an electric charge, and her charge level is her light radius. Spending charge to help someone visibly shrinks the world she can see. Resting visibly grows it back. No bar in a corner, no HUD lecture — the resource is the light you're seeing by. The maths carries the feeling." |
| 13:45–14:00 | End card: three panels — today's build, next episode's snow, the game frame. | "What exists now: a descent with honest light. Next episode, the water itself starts pushing — currents you can trust, and marine snow to ride them. And one breath of the real game: Volta's whole resource economy is this one equation, visible. See you in the current." |

## E14 · Currents you can trust

*Lesson skeleton: [lesson-2-currents-you-can-trust.md](lesson-2-currents-you-can-trust.md)
· Starter: [starter/current_field.gd](starter/current_field.gd) · Target ~13:30*

**Cold open (0:00–0:20).** The finished build: 200 flecks of marine snow
drifting on invisible currents, faint arrows leaning underneath, the whole
weather slowly turning. One line:

> "Nothing in this water is animated. All of it is moving."

**Beats.**

| Time | On screen | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0:20–1:00 | Presenter or voice over the drifting snow. | "Episode fourteen, Friendly Waters, part two. Last time we taught light to fade honestly. Today the water itself gets opinions: it will push, drift, and never dead-end. The promise: two hundred flecks of marine snow riding currents you built from one idea. Stopping point marked as always." |
| 1:00–2:10 | *B-roll: TV weather map with wind arrows; harbour water with drifting leaves.* | "Here's the idea, and it's the only one today: a vector field. Look at a weather map. There's a wind arrow over your town. And over the next town. And — this is the part to sit with — over every point *between* the towns. Infinitely many arrows, pinned to space itself. Nobody drew them all; the map is a rule that answers 'which way, how hard?' anywhere you ask. Water is that same map with the labels changed." |
| 2:10–3:10 | Maths box: F(x, y), n, θ; the pipeline noise → angle → arrow drawn as three stations. | "Three symbols. F — the field: the arrow at any point x, y. n — a noise value there, between minus one and one. Theta — the heading we make from it: n times a hundred and eighty degrees, so the full compass. Then pin a length-one arrow pointing that way. That's the entire machine: noise in, arrow out. One honest housekeeping note — in code, times one-eighty becomes times pi, because Godot speaks radians: same turn, different clothes." |
| 3:10–4:10 | Split screen: `randf()` field — porcupine static — versus smooth noise field. *B-roll: TV static.* | "Why noise and not plain randomness? Try it with randf and every arrow ignores its neighbours — static. A leaf dropped in static jitters in place; nothing can *travel*. Real currents are smooth: the arrow here and the arrow next door mostly agree. Randomness with manners. That's exactly what Ken Perlin built for computer graphics — 'An Image Synthesizer', SIGGRAPH, 1985 — and Godot ships a descendant of it built in, called FastNoiseLite. Forty years of hard work, handed to us as one object." |
| 4:10–5:10 | Live code, Step 1: the combed grid, arrows all east. | "Build time. Step one: pin arrows to space — a grid of little dashes with dots for heads, all pointing east. Combed water. The grid draws one arrow per sixty-four pixels, but remember what it *stands for*: an arrow at every point. We're sketching infinity, not exhausting it." |
| 5:10–6:20 | Step 2: FastNoiseLite created; `field_at()` on screen; run — the weather chart. Then live-tune NOISE_SCALE 0.001 → 0.01; change the seed twice. | "Step two: let noise choose the angles. One FastNoiseLite, one function — field_at: position in, unit arrow out. Run. A weather chart. And two knobs you should absolutely fiddle with right now: noise scale — small is broad lazy rivers, big is tight fussy eddies — and the seed. Every integer is a different sea. Pick a favourite; it's yours." |
| 6:20–7:20 | Step 3: one bright mote set loose; camera follows it through a curl. | "Step three: drop one mote in. Each frame it reads the arrow under its feet, takes that step, reads again. It has no plan and no engine — the journey belongs entirely to the field. The screen wraps like a doughnut: off the right edge, in from the left. Watch it commit to a bend it never chose. That's drift." |
| 7:20–8:20 | Step 4: two hundred motes. Long, quiet shot. | "Step four: arrays — the one mote becomes two hundred. And here's what you get free: specks that start near each other *travel* together, because they read nearly the same arrows. Currents make companions. Nobody coded that." |
| 8:20–9:10 | Step 5: sizes vary; everything sinks gently. *B-roll: real marine-snow footage, flecks falling in blacklit water.* | "Step five: name the thing. This is marine snow — real, by the way: a slow fall of organic flecks that feeds the deep; NOAA's education pages cover it. Two touches sell it: every fleck gets its own size, and everything settles downward a little, whatever the current says. If yours looks like a blizzard, turn the sink speed down. Tuning until water feels like water is real work." |
| 9:10–10:00 | Step 6: `get_noise_2d` becomes `get_noise_3d(x, y, time)`; the field visibly reconsiders itself. | "Last step, and it's sneaky-beautiful: ask the noise a 3D question and feed time in as the third axis. As time grows, the whole field eases from one weather into the next. No arrow ever snaps. Leave it running while you make tea; it never repeats and never breaks." |
| 10:00–10:35 | STOP HERE card over the drifting snow. | "You can stop here. Two hundred snow particles, drifting — the promise, kept. If your arrows are ugly but your snow drifts: you've finished. The arrows were scaffolding all along." |
| 10:35–12:05 | Go deeper: a corner where arrows point inward from all sides; snow piling like lint. Then the curl version: flow following the contours of a noise heightmap. *B-roll: weather-map isobars with wind running along them.* | "One honest flaw and one famous fix. The flaw: nothing in noise-to-angle promises that flow *out* of a region matches flow *in*. Some spots are sinks — arrows point inward all around, and snow piles up like lint. Real water can't do that; our field never took physics. The fix: don't read noise as the angle. Read the noise's *slope* — which way is uphill — and move sideways to it, a quarter-turn from the gradient. Flow then follows the contour lines of the noise-hills, like wind along isobars — and contours are closed loops, so nothing ever piles up. That's curl noise: Bridson, Hourihan and Nordenstam, SIGGRAPH 2007. The lesson has a drop-in function. And for the readable, free, long-form version of flow fields, Daniel Shiffman's Nature of Code — link below." |
| 12:05–13:15 | *Friendly Waters* mood frame: a current sweeping a small swimmer sideways off her line; she resumes from where the water put her. | "Why this matters to the game. Friendly Waters is full of currents, and here's its contract, stated once and kept everywhere: being swept costs you *position*, never *progress*. The water is allowed to be strong. It is not allowed to be cruel. No arrow in the ocean hurts anyone — the field pushes, carries, redirects. When we say the ocean has no health bar, this is what the water's half of that promise looks like." |
| 13:15–13:30 | End card: today's snow, next episode's school, the game frame. | "What exists now: water that pushes, drifts, and never dead-ends. Next episode is the series payoff — a school of fish that thinks together, with no leader, in three rules. And the game breath: in Friendly Waters, currents are geography, not punishment. Bring your field; the fish will swim in it." |

## E15 · A school that thinks together

*Lesson skeleton: [lesson-3-a-school-that-thinks-together.md](lesson-3-a-school-that-thinks-together.md)
· Starter: [starter/boids.gd](starter/boids.gd) · Target ~14:30*

**Cold open (0:00–0:20).** The finished school: sixty fish condensing out of
chaos, swelling, splitting around nothing, re-merging. One line:

> "Nobody is in charge of this. That's the lesson."

**Beats.**

| Time | On screen | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0:20–1:00 | Presenter or voice over the paused school. | "Final episode of Friendly Waters. We have light that fades honestly, water that pushes kindly. Today: company. The promise is a school of fish with no leader — sixty of them, three rules, and at the end, one shy extra who only appears if you've earned it. Stopping point marked, as always." |
| 1:00–2:15 | *B-roll: starling murmuration at dusk; baitball of schooling fish.* | "Watch a murmuration and your brain insists on a choreographer. There isn't one. No bird sees the whole flock. No fish sees the whole school. Each one watches a handful of nearest neighbours and keeps three small promises. Everything you're seeing — the swelling, the turning-as-one — is what those promises add up to. Nobody intends it." |
| 2:15–3:15 | Reynolds 1987 paper title page; the word "boids" typed large. | "This became computer science in 1987. Craig Reynolds, at SIGGRAPH, showed animated flocks built from three local rules and called the creatures 'boids'. His names: collision avoidance, velocity matching, flock centering. The field's nicknames, which we'll use: separation, alignment, cohesion. His paper is free on his own website — red3d.com — and it's genuinely readable. Link below." |
| 3:15–4:15 | Maths box: the wish equation with words inside it; r, v, w introduced. | "Here's the honest maths: boids has no grand equation — Reynolds published a *recipe*. Three symbols cover it. r: the neighbour radius — who counts as company. v: a fish's velocity. w: a weight — one volume knob per rule. Each rule proposes a direction; each proposal gets turned up or down by its knob; the fish adds them into one wish and *bends* toward it a little each frame. Bends — never snaps. That's why schools look muscled instead of magnetic." |
| 4:15–5:15 | Live code, Step 1: sixty triangles, random headings, doughnut wrapping. | "Step one: the cast before the play. Sixty positions, sixty random velocities, little triangles that point where they're going. Run: confetti that swims. Sixty strangers crossing a plaza. Remember this aimlessness — it's the 'before' photo, and the transformation takes exactly three steps." |
| 5:15–6:45 | Step 2: separation. The loop on screen; highlight the fresh-list line and the speed clamp. Run: polite swerves. | "Step two, rule one: personal space. Each fish collects a push away from anyone inside its bubble and bends toward the sum. Two lines of engine-room honesty come along. Every fish decides from the *same moment* — tomorrow's velocities go into a fresh list, so nobody reacts to a neighbour's future. And speeds get clamped to a sea-worthy range. Run it: subtle. Near-misses now resolve with a small swerve. If your fish twitch violently, you've probably made them *attract* intruders — one flipped sign, different game." |
| 6:45–7:45 | Step 3: alignment; NEIGHBOUR_RADIUS drawn as a circle around one fish for a beat. Run: lanes and rivers. | "Step three, rule two: swim the way we're swimming. A wider circle — earshot, seventy pixels — and each fish averages its neighbours' headings. Two proposals now, so the mixing desk appears: each rule gets a weight. Run: traffic! Lanes, rivers, agreement about direction. Beautiful — and not a school. They agree where to go. They feel no pull to be *together*." |
| 7:45–8:45 | Step 4: cohesion added. Run. Hold the shot as groups condense. | "Step four, rule three: don't leave me. Average the neighbours' positions — the middle of the local group — and drift toward it. Run... there it is. Groups condense out of traffic, recruit passers-by, split around nothing, re-merge. Search the file for the variable called 'school'. There isn't one. You built a *them* out of sixty *me*s." |
| 8:45–10:00 | Step 5: live mixing desk. Zero cohesion → traffic. Zero alignment → boiling clumps. Zero separation → the crystal. Radius 200 → one blob; 40 → shy parties. | "Now the part that makes you a designer rather than a physicist: play the desk. Cohesion to zero — motion without belonging. Alignment to zero — belonging without direction; clumps that boil in place. Separation to zero — belonging without respect; an uncomfortable crystal. Widen the radius and the whole tank becomes one consensus blob; shrink it, many small shy parties. There's no correct setting. There's only the school you meant." |
| 10:00–10:35 | STOP HERE card over the schooling school. | "You can stop here — the school is schooling, the promise is kept, and if yours clumps a bit oddly, you're ninety percent there: odd clumping means the rules are on. What's left is one optional guest." |
| 10:35–12:20 | Go deeper: fish zero drawn as five pink noise-scattered ghosts. On-screen: `amplitude = SHIMMER_PIXELS * (1 - trust)`. Holding →: the ghosts converge; at 1.0, one clear fish. *B-roll: translucent snailfish footage, pale against the dark.* | "Meet Baro. In Friendly Waters he's a hadal snailfish — and his real cousins are worth a sentence: gelatinous, translucent, soft-bodied, the deepest-living fishes known; one species was described from the Mariana Trench from depths approaching eight kilometres — Gerringer and colleagues, 2017, in Zootaxa. The game turns 'hard to see clearly' into a character: strangers can't quite see Baro, so he's drawn as several noise-scattered guesses, and the scatter's loudness is one minus trust. Watch: I hold right, trust climbs, the guesses agree... and at trust equals one, the noise falls silent. There he is. Nothing transformed — he was there the whole time. You stopped being a stranger. That's lesson two's noise and lesson three's school, holding hands." |
| 12:20–13:20 | *Friendly Waters* montage, store-page level: descent with the glow; currents; the school parting and reforming around a swimmer. | "Step back and look at the world you built in three episodes. Light as a budget you can see. Water that costs position, never progress. Company as three weighted kindnesses — and trust as *visibility*. Notice what's missing: no health bar, no damage numbers, nowhere in the ocean. The 'enemies' in Friendly Waters are misunderstandings, and the fix is the trust system, not a sword. The maths didn't decorate that design. The maths *is* that design." |
| 13:20–14:15 | The three builds side by side, then World 6 teaser frame: a runner, a ramp, a reaction-time ruler. | "Where next: world six is EXTR, run! — an endless runner that refuses to be mean. Speed ramps, reaction-time budgets, why v equals v-nought plus a-t decides what's *fair* before it decides what's fast. Different game, same house rule: the maths carries the kindness. And if you only ever built the school today — that counts. It always counts." |
| 14:15–14:30 | End card: the school, the shimmer resolving, the world-5 folder. | "What exists now: an ocean that behaves — light, water, and company, with no leader and no health bar. Next: motion that feels kind. And the game breath to leave on: in Friendly Waters, being seen clearly is something friends do to you. Turn trust up. Watch him appear." |
